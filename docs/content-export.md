## 1. Home

### Exploring Values

*A Philosophical Experiment by Justin Tauber*

> Exploring Values — A philosophical experiment on human values

This site is an interactive exploration of human values—what we care about, why it matters, and how different value priorities create both harmony and conflict. Using Schwartz's scientifically validated framework, you can map value profiles, discover tensions, and explore how values express themselves through everyday stressors like money, time, and attention.

### The Schwartz Theory of Basic Values

Shalom Schwartz's theory identifies 19 universal values that exist across all human cultures. These values—from Security and Tradition to Stimulation and Self-Direction—are arranged in a circular structure where adjacent values are compatible and opposing values are in tension.

The theory has been validated across 80+ countries and hundreds of thousands of participants, making it one of the most robust cross-cultural frameworks for understanding what humans fundamentally care about. The circular structure reveals a deeper truth: pursuing some values necessarily comes at the cost of others.

#### The 19 Values by Higher-Order Grouping

**Openness to Change** — Independent thought and action, readiness for new experiences

- **SDT — Self-direction – thought**: Freedom to cultivate one's own ideas and abilities
- **SDA — Self-direction – action**: Freedom to determine one's own actions and plans
- **STI — Stimulation**: Excitement, novelty, and challenge in life
- **HED — Hedonism**: Pleasure and sensuous gratification for oneself

**Self-Enhancement** — Personal success and dominance over others

- **ACM — Achievement**: Success according to social standards
- **POD — Power – dominance**: Power through exercising control over people
- **POR — Power – resources**: Power through control of material and social resources
- **FAC — Face**: Maintaining one's public image and avoiding humiliation

**Conservation** — Self-restriction, order, and resistance to change

- **SEO — Security – personal**: Safety in one's immediate environment
- **SES — Security – societal**: Safety and stability in the wider society
- **TRD — Tradition**: Maintaining and preserving cultural, family, or religious traditions
- **COR — Conformity – rules**: Compliance with rules, laws, and formal obligations
- **COI — Conformity – interpersonal**: Avoidance of upsetting or harming other people
- **HUM — Humility**: Recognizing one's insignificance in the larger scheme of things

**Self-Transcendence** — Concern for the welfare and interests of others

- **BEC — Benevolence – caring**: Devotion to the welfare of in-group members
- **BED — Benevolence – dependability**: Being a reliable and trustworthy member of the in-group
- **UNC — Universalism – concern**: Commitment to equality, justice, and protection for all people
- **UNN — Universalism – nature**: Preservation of the natural environment
- **UNT — Universalism – tolerance**: Acceptance and understanding of those who are different

### Tensions & Stressors

**Value Tensions**

Not all values can be maximized simultaneously. Security opposes Stimulation. Self-Enhancement conflicts with Self-Transcendence. Understanding these tensions helps explain interpersonal conflicts and internal dilemmas—they're not failures of character, but fundamental trade-offs in what we can prioritize.

**Stressors**

Stressors are forms of scarcity or constraint that force tradeoffs. They make latent value differences behaviorally visible by empowering or antagonising values in different ways.

## 2. Stressors

> Stressors & Polarity — Decision-space primitives that expose value tensions

### Understanding Stressors

**Motivation Space**

**Schwartz values** describe what people care about — their underlying motivations. But values alone don't predict behavior.

**Decision Space**

**Stressors** are forms of scarcity or constraint that force tradeoffs. They make latent value differences behaviorally visible.

**Why Stressors Matter**

Without a relevant stressor, two people with very different values may behave identically — no scarcity means no forced choice. Stressors are the "pressure" that reveals what people truly prioritize.

**Important Caveats**

Research supports that situational features systematically generate value tension. However:

- Stressor effects are **probabilistic, not deterministic** — individual differences in identity, experience, and coping resources moderate their impact
- The 12 stressors are **analytical lenses, not independent variables** — in practice, they overlap and correlate
- Effects are **culturally and institutionally mediated** — authority and autonomy tensions differ across contexts (healthcare vs. military vs. startups)

*Read the full research background → (see section 4)*

### The 12 Stressors

Each stressor below lists its description, a concrete example, and the parameters that can be tuned to intensify or soften it (shown as *low label — parameter name — high label*).

#### 1. Risk / Uncertainty

The degree of unknown outcomes, potential loss, or unpredictable consequences. High risk forces tradeoffs between security-seeking and opportunity-seeking values.

*Example:* A startup founder must decide whether to leave a stable job to pursue an unproven business idea with uncertain chances of success.

- Low stakes — **Stakes Level** — Existential stakes
- Easily reversible — **Reversibility** — Permanent/irreversible
- Full information — **Information Availability** — Deep uncertainty

#### 2. Control / Authority

Who has decision-making power and how it is distributed. Creates tension between autonomy-seeking and hierarchy-respecting values.

*Example:* A team member disagrees with their manager's directive and must decide whether to voice concerns or follow instructions.

- Flat/ambiguous — **Hierarchy Clarity** — Strict hierarchy
- Limited domain — **Control Scope** — Total control
- Questioned legitimacy — **Perceived Legitimacy** — Unquestioned authority

#### 3. Resources / Allocation

Scarcity of material goods, money, or tangible assets. Forces choices between self-interest and collective welfare.

*Example:* A bonus pool must be divided among team members who contributed unequally to a successful project.

- Abundant — **Scarcity Level** — Severely scarce
- Easily shared — **Divisibility** — Indivisible
- Private allocation — **Allocation Visibility** — Public/transparent

#### 4. Time / Urgency

Pressure from deadlines, windows of opportunity, or time-sensitive demands. Forces tradeoffs between deliberation and action.

*Example:* A job offer expires in 48 hours, but you're still waiting to hear back from your dream company.

- Open-ended — **Deadline Pressure** — Immediate deadline
- Recurring opportunity — **Opportunity Window** — Once-in-a-lifetime
- Single focus — **Competing Demands** — Multiple urgent demands

#### 5. Attention / Recognition

Visibility, credit, reputation, and social acknowledgment. Creates tension between self-promotion and humility values.

*Example:* You completed most of the work on a group project, but the presentation slot only allows one person to present to leadership.

- Private — **Audience Size** — Mass public
- Ephemeral — **Record Permanence** — Permanent record
- Collective/anonymous — **Attribution Clarity** — Individual spotlight

#### 6. Norm Enforcement / Rule Flexibility

How strictly rules, laws, and social norms are applied. Forces tradeoffs between conformity and self-direction.

*Example:* A compassionate exception to a company policy would help one employee but set a precedent that others may exploit.

- Flexible interpretation — **Enforcement Strictness** — Zero tolerance
- Minor consequences — **Sanction Severity** — Severe punishment
- Ambiguous norms — **Norm Clarity** — Explicit rules

#### 7. Choice Freedom / Constraint

The range of available options and freedom to choose among them. Exposes tension between autonomy and security.

*Example:* An employee is offered a promotion that requires relocating to another country—accept or stay in current role.

- Binary choice — **Option Range** — Many options
- No exit — **Exit Possibility** — Easy exit
- Free choice — **Coercion Level** — Forced choice

#### 8. Inclusion / Exclusion

Who belongs, who is accepted, and who is left out. Forces tradeoffs between in-group loyalty and universal concern.

*Example:* A social group must decide whether to invite a controversial but talented newcomer who might disrupt group harmony.

- Open to all — **Group Selectivity** — Highly exclusive
- Quiet exclusion — **Rejection Visibility** — Public rejection
- Low stakes — **Stakes of Belonging** — Survival-level stakes

#### 9. Truth Disclosure / Concealment

Decisions about revealing or hiding information. Creates tension between honesty and protection values.

*Example:* A doctor must decide how much to tell a patient about a grim prognosis when the family requests optimism.

- Harmless truth — **Harm Potential** — Devastating revelation
- Unlikely to surface — **Discovery Likelihood** — Certain to emerge
- No obligation — **Obligation Strength** — Absolute duty

#### 10. Effort / Sacrifice

Personal cost in energy, comfort, or wellbeing required by a choice. Exposes tension between self-care and commitment values.

*Example:* A parent must choose between attending their child's recital or staying late to meet a critical work deadline.

- Minimal effort — **Personal Cost** — Extreme sacrifice
- Self/close others — **Beneficiary Distance** — Distant strangers
- Guaranteed return — **Reciprocity Expectation** — No reciprocity

#### 11. Change / Stability

Tension between preserving the status quo and embracing transformation. Exposes openness vs. conservation values.

*Example:* A traditional family business must decide whether to modernize operations or preserve practices passed down for generations.

- Minor adjustment — **Disruption Scope** — Total transformation
- Recent practice — **Tradition Depth** — Ancient tradition
- Easily reversed — **Change Reversibility** — Permanent change

#### 12. Boundary Permeability

Who counts as "us" — the flexibility of in-group/out-group boundaries. Forces tradeoffs between universalism and particularism.

*Example:* A community must decide whether to share limited emergency resources with neighboring towns during a crisis.

- Fluid boundaries — **Boundary Rigidity** — Impermeable walls
- Distant/abstract — **Outsider Proximity** — Present/concrete
- Identity irrelevant — **Identity Salience** — Identity defining

### Polarity Heatmap

Each cell shows how increasing a stressor's intensity tends to **satisfy (+)** or **frustrate (−)** a value.

Polarity scores range from −1.0 to +1.0:

- **+1.0** = Increasing this stressor strongly **satisfies** the value
- **+0.5** = Increasing this stressor moderately satisfies the value
- **0.0** = The stressor is orthogonal / weakly related to the value
- **−0.5** = Increasing this stressor moderately **frustrates** the value
- **−1.0** = Increasing this stressor strongly frustrates the value

## 3. Values as Preferred Verbs

> Values as Preferred Verbs — Extending Schwartz's theory through predicate forms

Schwartz's PVQ-RR describes values as abstract motivational goals — what a person fundamentally cares about. But goals only become visible through action. By expressing each of the 19 basic values as first-person verb forms, we can move from the abstract to the behavioural: not just "this person values Benevolence" but "this person cares, helps, nurtures."

This is a proposed extension of the theory, not a standard component of it. The verb forms below are interpretive — designed to be evocative rather than exhaustive, and to make value profiles readable as portraits of agency.

### The 19 Schwartz Basic Values as Verbs

| Basic value | Preferred predicate forms |
|---|---|
| **SDT** Self-direction – thought | I analyse / I imagine / I question / I infer / I understand |
| **SDA** Self-direction – action | I choose / I initiate / I decide / I try / I act independently |
| **STI** Stimulation | I explore / I experiment / I seek / I dare / I discover |
| **HED** Hedonism | I enjoy / I savour / I indulge / I relish / I experience pleasure |
| **ACM** Achievement | I accomplish / I excel / I demonstrate competence / I succeed / I deliver |
| **POD** Power – dominance | I direct / I command / I control / I overrule / I dominate |
| **POR** Power – resources | I acquire / I possess / I allocate / I command resources / I accumulate |
| **FAC** Face | I impress / I avoid embarrassment / I maintain appearances / I preserve standing |
| **SEO** Security – personal | I protect myself / I avoid danger / I secure / I safeguard / I reduce risk |
| **SES** Security – societal | I stabilise / I defend / I enforce order / I preserve safety / I maintain security |
| **TRD** Tradition | I preserve / I honour / I continue / I observe / I inherit |
| **COR** Conformity – rules | I comply / I obey / I follow / I uphold / I refrain |
| **COI** Conformity – interpersonal | I avoid upsetting others / I accommodate / I defer / I restrain myself / I show respect |
| **HUM** Humility | I defer / I accept my place / I avoid self-assertion / I remain modest |
| **BEC** Benevolence – caring | I care / I help / I support / I comfort / I nurture |
| **BED** Benevolence – dependability | I fulfil obligations / I show up / I keep promises / I take responsibility / I remain reliable |
| **UNC** Universalism – concern | I protect / I defend / I support / I alleviate suffering / I empower |
| **UNN** Universalism – nature | I preserve / I conserve / I protect ecosystems / I restore / I steward |
| **UNT** Universalism – tolerance | I include / I listen / I accept / I accommodate difference / I respect diversity |

### Three Lenses: Tense and Modality

The same verb changes meaning depending on how it is inflected — revealing different aspects of a person's relationship to a value.

**Character** *(Present simple)*
The values I already act from, habitually and reliably. This is who I am — not what I aspire to or what my role requires, but what consistently animates my choices and attention.
*"I relish."*

**Preference** *(Future simple)*
The experiences I'm drawn toward and would choose given the opportunity. This register captures desire and intention — what I would do if unconstrained by circumstance or duty.
*"I will relish."*

**Role** *(Modal — should)*
The obligations my position places on me. This register is normative — it may or may not reflect intrinsic motivation, but it describes what a role demands and what social context reinforces.
*"I should relish."*

**Why the gaps between lenses matter**

When the three forms align — "I care / I will care / I should care" — the value is integrated: it is simultaneously who the person is, what they want, and what their role demands.

Gaps are diagnostically interesting. "I should comply" without "I will comply" may signal external constraint without internalized motivation. "I will analyse" without "I should analyse" may describe intellectual curiosity that exists outside the demands of a person's current role. Conflict between registers is often where value tension becomes most visible.

### Exploring Two Archetypes

Leslie Knope and Spock share high scores on Self-direction–thought and Achievement, but diverge sharply elsewhere. Reading their top values across all three lenses makes the contrast vivid.

**Leslie Knope** — A relentlessly driven public servant whose ambition and genuine love for her community are inseparable. Combines extraordinary self-direction with deep care for others — every project is both a personal mission and a legacy for Pawnee.

**Spock** — A logical mind who values truth and duty over emotion. Prioritizes intellectual self-direction while maintaining conformity to principles.

**Reading the contrast**

Both archetypes act from Self-direction–thought — "I analyse / I imagine" — but arrive at it differently. For Spock, it sits alongside Conformity–rules ("I comply / I obey") and Humility ("I defer / I accept my place"). For Knope, it sits alongside Benevolence–caring ("I care / I help") and Achievement ("I accomplish / I excel"). The same verb — "I analyse" — belongs to two very different characters.

The role lens is particularly revealing. "I should comply" and "I should defer" describe a character whose obligations run inward — toward principle and self-restraint. "I should care" and "I should accomplish" describe someone whose obligations run outward — toward community and results. Same tense; entirely different moral worlds.

## 4. Research Background

> Research Background — Literature review of the stressors framework

**About This Review**

This page presents a research-grounded evaluation of the "stressors" concept used on this site. It reviews empirical and theoretical support, challenges and critiques, and comparable taxonomies from psychology, organizational studies, and moral philosophy. The term "stressors" refers to situational features that systematically activate, strain, or force trade-offs among values—rather than being values themselves.

Below is a structured summary of the relevant academic literature, organized into three sections: research that supports the idea of stressors, research that challenges or complicates the framework, and comparable taxonomies that provide useful context.

### 1. Research That Supports Stressors

Across psychology, organizational studies, and moral philosophy, there is strong convergent support for the claim that specific situational properties reliably generate value tension.

#### 1.1 Moral Stress, Moral Uncertainty, and Value Strain

Research on moral stress shows that tension arises not merely from conflicting values, but from situational ambiguity, responsibility, and constraint—precisely the kind of "stressor" the stressors taxonomy highlights.

- **(Reynolds et al., 2012)** define moral stress as arising from uncertainty, ambiguity, and responsibility under constraint, emphasizing that stress is situationally induced rather than value-intrinsic.
- **(Larsson et al., 2018)** show that acute situations involving moral challenges (time pressure, stakes, role obligation) reliably produce stress reactions even when individuals' core values remain stable.

*These findings support the idea that tension is "carried" by situational features, not by values alone.*

#### 1.2 Value Conflict and Situational Activation

Values research consistently shows that values are trans-situational, but conflict is situationally triggered.

- **(Bouckenooghe et al., 2005)** demonstrate that stress is predicted not by values per se, but by value conflict activated by organizational and situational demands.
- **(Lee, 2015)** explicitly frames managerial dilemmas as situational tensions acting on stable value systems, arguing that values become problematic only under certain contextual pressures.

*This aligns strongly with the stressor metaphor: values are latent; situations load them.*

#### 1.3 Moral Dilemmas and Situational Factor Taxonomies

There is direct precedent for classifying dilemmas by situational features, rather than moral content.

- **(Klenk et al., 2022)** reviews how factors like personal force, proximity, uncertainty, and outcome salience systematically alter moral judgment across dilemmas.
- **(Myyry et al., 2007)** propose a taxonomy of real-life moral problems based on socio-cognitive conflict intensity and emotional load.

*These works strongly support the legitimacy of a structured list of situational "tension amplifiers."*

### 2. Research That Challenges the Framework

#### 2.1 Individual Differences Undermine Fixed Stressor Effects

One challenge is that the same situational feature does not produce the same tension for all agents.

- **(Carminati et al., 2023)** show that moral identity strength and role identification moderate whether a situation produces conflict or coherence.
- **(Haan et al., 1975)** found large divergences between hypothetical and actual moral reasoning depending on identity, experience, and coping resources.

**Implication:** Tension stressors are probabilistic, not deterministic; they interact with person-level variables.

#### 2.2 Overlap and Non-Orthogonality

Organizational stress research repeatedly finds that situational stressors are highly correlated.

- **(Jex et al., 2003)** show that role conflict, ambiguity, and constraints overlap strongly and resist clean separation.
- **(Fida et al., 2015)** argue for integrative rather than modular models of stressors because discrete categories often collapse empirically.

**Implication:** A list of 12 stressors risks false precision unless explicitly framed as analytical lenses rather than independent variables.

#### 2.3 Cultural and Institutional Dependence

What counts as a tension-stressor is culturally mediated.

- Situations involving authority, hierarchy, or autonomy produce different value tensions across institutional contexts (e.g., healthcare vs. military vs. startups) **(Larsson et al., 2018)**.

**Implication:** This challenges any claim of universality without contextual qualifiers.

### 3. Comparable Taxonomies

The stressors framework sits at the intersection of several established classification traditions:

#### 3.1 Role Stressor Models *(Organizational Psychology)*

- Role conflict
- Role ambiguity
- Role overload

*(Jex et al., 2003)*

These overlap strongly with stressors like conflicting obligations, uncertainty, and resource constraints.

#### 3.2 Moral Dilemma Feature Taxonomies *(Moral Psychology)*

- Personal force
- Proximity
- Intentionality
- Outcome certainty

*(Klenk et al., 2022)*

These are near-isomorphic to several proposed stressors and provide empirical grounding.

#### 3.3 Value Conflict Frameworks *(Values Research)*

- Intrapersonal vs. interpersonal conflict
- Value hierarchy clashes

*(Bouckenooghe et al., 2005; Lee, 2015)*

These focus less on situations, but help explain why stressors produce strain.

#### 3.4 Socio-Cognitive Conflict Models *(Social Psychology)*

- Degree of social disagreement
- Emotional salience
- Cognitive complexity

*(Myyry et al., 2007)*

These are especially relevant if stressors include social exposure, contestation, or public accountability.

### Overall Assessment

**What the Research Supports**

- The core idea that situational features systematically generate value tension is very well supported.
- Treating these features as a taxonomy of stressors is conceptually legitimate and empirically defensible.

**What the Research Challenges**

- Fixed, universal effects of any single stressor
- Strict independence between stressors
- Culture-free or role-free interpretations

**Best Framing Going Forward**

The strongest alignment with the literature is to treat the 12 stressors as:

> "Recurring situational patterns that probabilistically activate value conflict, moderated by identity, role, and context."
